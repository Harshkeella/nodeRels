Okay, let's dive into optimizing with gradient descent. You see, unlike linear regression, where we have a closed-form solution for θ, here we need to learn the parameters iteratively. This means we'll be making small adjustments to our parameters until we find the best fit.

Now, to understand how we do this, let's look at the function J(θ). We need to differentiate J(θ) with respect to each parameter θⱼ. To do this, we use the chain rule and the derivative of the sigmoid function, which we learned earlier: σ'(z) equals σ(z) times (1 minus σ(z)).

When we apply this, something magical happens. All the log and exponential terms cancel out, leaving us with a form that's very similar to what we see in linear regression. Specifically, the result is the average, over all examples, of the prediction error (h_θ(x) minus y) scaled by the feature value xⱼ.

So, for each parameter θⱼ, we take a small step in the direction that reduces the error. The size of this step is controlled by the learning rate α. All the parameters are updated simultaneously, with each iteration bringing our predictions closer to the true labels.

And that's the key idea behind gradient descent: we keep making small adjustments to our parameters until the function J(θ) converges to its minimum. This process helps us find the best values for our parameters, making our model as accurate as possible.
